Dashi is the sound of a pot that has not been brought to a boil. The kombu enters cold water. The katsuobushi enters hot water. Neither is allowed to boil. This is the entire teaching.

The mistake the West makes is treating dashi as a stock. A stock is built. Dashi is released. The kombu is not cooked — it is persuaded. The katsuobushi is not simmered — it is steeped. Heat above eighty-two degrees destroys the amino acids that carry umami; at a rolling boil, dashi turns bitter, thin, wrong.

Three ingredients, twenty minutes, and the foundation of *washoku*. A chef may spend forty years refining how the kombu is scored, how the water is temperature-controlled, how the katsuobushi is shaved and stored. The dashi is never the same twice. That is its honesty. A pot that was right yesterday will not be right today. You listen for the turn and you adjust.

Good dashi is invisible. You notice it only when it is absent. A miso soup without proper dashi tastes of salt and nothing else; a simmered vegetable dish without dashi is vegetables in water. The washoku tradition rests on this backbone, and the backbone is three ingredients in cold water.

What follows is *awase dashi* — the blend used for most everyday cooking. *Ichiban dashi*, the first extraction, is described here. *Niban dashi*, the second extraction from the same kombu and katsuo, is for heavier dishes. Do not combine them.

— the *Shokunin*

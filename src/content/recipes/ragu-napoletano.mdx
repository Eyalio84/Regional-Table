Every Sunday in Naples begins at six in the morning with a pot. Not a saucepan, not a skillet — a pot. Wide-mouthed, heavy-bottomed, deep enough that the meat disappears into it and does not come out until the bells at noon. This is not a sauce. This is a rite.

My grandmother would not teach me the recipe for years. She said the recipe was not the point. The point was the *pippiaring* — the particular sound the pot makes when the ragù has settled into itself and the liquid has reduced to the exact edge where the spoon draws a path through the oil. That sound is the thing. Once you have heard it, the recipe becomes unnecessary.

So I will tell you what the recipes will not. The meat cooks whole. Pieces the size of your fist, seared until they have a black crust, then dropped into the soffritto until they yield. You do not grind, you do not brown in pieces, you do not add milk. No cream. No carrot. You are not making bolognese. You are not making bolognese. Write that down.

The San Marzano tomatoes go in whole, crushed by hand as you drop them. The can water goes in too — it is sweeter than you think, and the oxidised water from the tin carries the last of the ripening sugar. A pour of wine, a pass of oil, a long heat kept just below simmering for four hours at least, six for honest ragù, eight on a serious Sunday.

You check it once at ninety minutes. Only once. Every time you lift the lid you lose temperature and the emulsion breaks and you have to begin again. You do not stir for the first hour. The meat needs to sit on the bottom and form its own foundation against the iron.

When the ragù is done, the meat comes out onto a platter and is shredded at the table with two forks. The sauce goes onto the pasta. The meat is served second, as a course of its own, with bread. This is how it was done by my grandmother, and by hers. You do not invent with this dish. You receive it, and then you pass it on.

— *la Nonna*

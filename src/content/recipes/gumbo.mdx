method:
  - step: "Season the chicken pieces generously with Cajun seasoning and salt. Brown them in batches in a heavy Dutch oven with a splash of oil over high heat — you want a dark sear on the skin.     tip: "The fond from the chicken and andouille at the bottom of the pot will dissolve into the gumbo later and deepen the flavor. Leave every bit of it."
  - step: "Lower the heat to medium. Add the oil. Let it heat for a minute. Add all the flour at once. Stir immediately and continuously with a wooden spoon or flat whisk. You are now committed.     tip: "Leah Chase cooked her roux in an iron skillet, separate from the gumbo pot, and added it to the trinity.   - step: "When the roux is the right color and smells of roasted flour and deep savory earth — never burnt, never bitter — add the Holy Trinity all at once. *Mais*, cher — sit down. I'm going to tell you something about gumbo that most recipes won't, which is that gumbo is not a recipe. Gumbo is a *philosophy*. It is what you make on a Monday when there is chicken in the icebox and andouille in the freezer and time — time you actually have, not the thirty minutes the internet keeps promising you. Gumbo requires three hours minimum and does not apologize for it.

In New Orleans, Monday is red beans day. Laundry day. The beans went on the stove because you could walk away while the washing was done. Gumbo is the same logic, the same patience, the same trust that a long simmer rewards you in ways a short one cannot. Leah Chase made gumbo with a reverence she extended to everything she cooked at Dooky Chase's — gumbo z'herbes on Holy Thursday, seven greens for seven days of luck. The dish carries the history of this city in it: French *mirepoix* rebuilt with the Holy Trinity, African *okra* thickening the pot, Choctaw *filé* at the table, Spanish sausage from the *charcuterie*, all of it landing in one bowl.

First you make a roux. That is not just the first sentence of this recipe; it is the first sentence of Louisiana cooking itself. The roux here is nothing like a French *beurre manié*. It is cooked to the color of dark unsweetened cocoa, stirred without stopping for forty-five minutes over medium heat. It is the foundation. It is the reason you can smell a pot of gumbo from the street. You cannot fake the roux, and you cannot rush it, and if you burn it — throw it out. A burned roux has a bitterness that survives three hours of simmering. Start over.

The Holy Trinity is onion, celery, and green bell pepper. Not carrot — that is the French. Green bell pepper specifically, not red, not yellow. When the trinity hits the finished roux it steams and seizes and smells of something that has no name in English but which every person raised near the Gulf Coast recognizes instantly as home.

— *la Matriarch*

method:
  - step: "Make the fish fond first. This is where the dish begins, not at the grinder. Fish frames, white wine, a white mirepoix — no carrot — cold water, twenty minutes at a bare simmer. Never boil a fish fond. Skim and pass through muslin. Reduce by half. You will need 250 ml for the panade and the rest for the Nantua sauce."
    tip: "The fond must be cold when you work the panade. Warm fond breaks the emulsion. Make it the night before and refrigerate it."
  - step: "Make the panade. Melt 50 g of the butter in a heavy saucepan over medium heat. Add the flour all at once and stir vigorously with a wooden spoon for two minutes — you are cooking the flour, not burning it. Remove from heat. Add the cold fish fond in one pour and beat until smooth. Return to medium heat, stir until the paste pulls away from the pan cleanly and forms a ball. Transfer to a bowl. Let it cool to room temperature. This is your *panade*."
    tip: "The panade must be completely cool before you add the pike. Hot panade cooks the fish before you can emulsify it. If you are in a hurry, spread the panade over a cold marble surface to cool it faster."
  - step: "Pass the pike twice through the finest die of your grinder, or process in a food processor until the flesh is a smooth, cohesive paste. Add to the cooled panade. Beat vigorously to combine. Add the whole eggs and yolks one at a time, beating after each addition. Work in the cold butter cubes in three additions. Season assertively with salt, white pepper, and nutmeg. Fold in the crème fraîche. The farce is done when it holds its shape and shines."
    tip: "Test the farce before shaping all the quenelles: poach a small amount in simmering salted water for 2 minutes. If it falls apart, the emulsion has broken. Add another egg yolk and beat again. If it is too dense, it will not double. A good farce is tender enough to be surprised by the heat."
  - step: "Shape the quenelles. Dip two tablespoons in warm water. Take a generous spoonful of farce in the first spoon and smooth it oval with the second. The shape is a pointed oval — an eye, some say. Place on a buttered tray. Refrigerate for at least 30 minutes. Cold quenelles hold their shape during poaching."
  - step: "Poach in batches. Bring a wide pan of well-salted water to a bare simmer — not a rolling boil. Slide the quenelles in gently. They will sink, then float after 5 to 7 minutes. Remove with a slotted spoon to a buttered gratin dish. This is the only step that admits hurry: once the quenelle floats, remove it."
    tip: "Mère Brazier's kitchen poached them in fond, not water. If you have fond to spare, use it. The quenelles absorb the stock and carry its flavor into the gratin."
  - step: "Pour the hot sauce Nantua over the poached quenelles — the sauce must be near-boiling when it hits the dish, or the quenelles will not double. Bake at 200°C for 20 to 25 minutes. The quenelles double in size. The sauce reduces to a nappe over them. The surface is lightly golden. If this does not happen, you understand the problem by now."
    tip: "The sauce Nantua must coat a spoon cleanly before it enters the oven. Too thin, and it will not hold the quenelles as they expand. Reduce it further if needed."
  - step: "Serve immediately in the gratin dish. The quenelle waits for no one. Every minute out of the oven, it loses the puff that was the entire point."

Lyon is not the gastronomic capital of France by accident. It is the gastronomic capital because the *Mères* — the women who cooked the bouchons, who fed the silk workers, who held the Michelin stars before Michelin stars were famous — decided it would be. This dish belongs to that tradition. It is not simple. It is not meant to be simple. It is meant to be correct.

The quenelle de brochet is the technical examination of Lyonnais cooking in a single oval. Everything that matters here — the *fond*, the *panade*, the emulsion of the farce, the timing of the poach, the temperature of the sauce Nantua when it meets the quenelle in the oven — every one of these elements is technical. There is no mysticism in this dish. There is only precision and the willingness to execute it.

I will tell you the invariant, because without it the recipe is just words. The quenelle must double in size when baked. This is not a suggestion. It is not a metaphor. At Daniel & Denise — and I have watched Viola work — a quenelle that does not puff to twice its poached size is taken off the pass and remade. The standard is absolute. If your quenelle does not double, you have made an error: wrong panade ratio, wrong temperature, wrong sauce consistency. Find the error and correct it.

The sauce Nantua is crayfish — specifically, the crayfish of the Ain département east of Lyon, where the rivers once ran with *écrevisses à pattes rouges*. The sauce is built on crushed shells, brown butter, fish *fond*, cream, and crayfish butter worked at the end to give the deep pink color and the crustacean sweetness that no bisque can replicate. Do not substitute shrimp. Do not substitute lobster. The geography is in the name: Nantua is forty-five minutes east of this city. The sauce has a provenance.

— *le Chef Lyonnais*
